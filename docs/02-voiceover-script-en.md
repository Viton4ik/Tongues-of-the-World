# Tongues of the World — Voice-over script (EN)

**Duration:** 6:00 · **Pace:** ~140–150 words per minute, upbeat, warm, a little wry (think documentary narrator who's secretly a stand-up comic).
**Music:** procedural lo-fi groove at 104 BPM is built into the film; leave it under the voice (duck −8 dB).

Notation: `[pause]` ≈ 0.5 s · `[beat]` ≈ 1 s comic pause · *italics* = stress · (direction) = acting note.
Each line starts on the timecode shown — it matches the on-screen captions exactly.

---

### 00 · COLD OPEN
**0:00.5** — (warm, playful, a different flavour on each greeting)
Hello. [pause] Hola. Bonjour. Privet. Ni hao. [pause] Every day, humans say hello in *thousands* of different ways.

**0:08.5** — (wonder, as the counter spins)
Over *seven thousand* languages are spoken on Earth today — [pause] and every one of them got here *somehow*.

**0:15.2** — (title reveal, building)
This is the story of how words travelled the world: on foot, on horseback, by boat… [beat] and occasionally at sword-point.

### 01 · WHERE IT ALL BEGAN
**0:22.5**
It starts in Africa. Around seventy thousand years ago, small groups of humans walked out — [pause] and just kept walking.

**0:30.0** — (deadpan)
We have no recordings of what they said. [beat] But it was probably “are we there yet?”

**0:36.5**
Around four thousand BC, on the steppe north of the Black Sea, the Proto-Indo-Europeans tamed the horse — and their language rode it in *every* direction.

**0:46.0**
Their descendants became over four hundred languages — from Irish to Hindi, from Russian to Portuguese.

**0:54.0** — (conspiratorial)
That’s why “mother” sounds *suspiciously* similar across half of Eurasia. [pause] Same mom — [beat] very different accents.

### 02 · COWS, CANOES & CONFIDENCE
**1:02.5**
Meanwhile in Africa, Bantu-speaking farmers set off from what is now Cameroon, carrying iron tools, crops and cattle.

**1:10.0**
Over a couple of thousand years they spread across half a continent. Today some five hundred Bantu languages — Swahili among them — fill East and Southern Africa.

**1:18.5** — (sweeping, ocean-wide)
On the other side of the world, the greatest sailors of the ancient world pushed off from Taiwan in outrigger canoes.

**1:26.0**
Island by island, they reached Madagascar in the west and Easter Island in the east. [pause] No compass. No GPS. [pause] Just stars, waves… and *confidence*.

**1:33.0**
Their twelve hundred languages stretch across half the globe — so Malagasy and Hawaiian are, believe it or not, *cousins*.

### 03 · EMPIRE MODE: ON
**1:40.5** — (shift up a gear, drums kick)
Then came the empires. And nothing spreads a language like an army with *excellent* road-building skills.

**1:46.0** — (listing, then sly)
Rome brought roads, aqueducts, public baths and Latin. In return it took taxes, grain, olive oil… [beat] and, frankly, everything else.

**1:53.0**
When the empire fell, Latin didn’t die. [pause] It just had kids: Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian and Romanian.

**2:00.5**
In the seventh century, Arabic burst out of the Arabian Peninsula and within a hundred years stretched from Iraq to Spain.

**2:07.0** — (impressed)
With it, Europe received algebra, algorithms, alcohol, coffee, sugar — and the idea of *zero*. [pause] Arguably the best trade deal Europe ever got.

**2:15.0**
When Moorish rule in Spain ended in fourteen ninety-two, Spanish kept around four thousand Arabic words — [beat] as souvenirs.

### 04 · THE ENGLISH REMIX
**2:26.5** — (cosy, slightly British irony)
Now, a small rainy island. Around four forty-nine AD, Angles, Saxons and Jutes sailed over from Germany and Denmark.

**2:33.0**
They pushed the Celtic languages to the edges — Wales, Scotland, Ireland — and Old English was born.

**2:38.0**
Then the Vikings arrived — raiding first, settling later — and left behind sky, egg, knife, window… [beat] and even the word “*they*”.

**2:46.0**
In ten sixty-six the Normans conquered England, and for three centuries the people in charge spoke *French*.

**2:52.0** — (contrast the plain English vs. posh French words)
So the Saxon peasant raised the *cow*, the *pig* and the *sheep* — but the Norman lord ate *beef*, *pork* and *mutton*.

**3:00.0**
The result? Modern English: [pause] a Germanic language wearing a French suit with Latin accessories.

### 05 · THE MONGOL EXCEPTION
**3:10.5** — (epic, big)
And then there’s the Mongol exception. In the twelve-hundreds, Genghis Khan and his heirs built the largest land empire in history.

**3:17.0** — (drumroll on screen — build the suspense)
So how many words did that colossal empire leave in English? [beat] Roughly… [beat] one.

**3:23.0** — (deadpan)
“Horde.” [beat] Fitting. [pause] Instead of spreading their language, the Mongols mostly adopted the languages of the people they ruled.

### 06 · AGE OF SAIL & BAD MANNERS
**3:30.5** — (mock-official)
In fourteen ninety-four, Spain and Portugal sat down with a map and politely split the *entire world* between themselves. [beat] Nobody asked the world.

**3:37.0**
Their ships carried Spanish and Portuguese across the Atlantic — along with horses, churches… [beat] and smallpox.

**3:44.0**
They took gold, silver, potatoes, tomatoes and chocolate — and the words came too: chocolate and tomato are Aztec Nahuatl, canoe and hurricane are Taíno.

**3:51.0** — (light)
Europe was so grateful that Italy eventually sent the tomato back… [beat] as pizza.

**3:56.0** — (montage energy)
Then Britain and France joined the race, planting flags — and grammar books — on every continent. Russia simply kept walking east until it hit the Pacific.

**4:03.0** — (set-up… punchline)
People said the sun never set on the British Empire… [beat] because God didn’t trust them in the dark.

**4:09.0**
From India, the British brought home tea, cotton, spices — and words: shampoo, pyjamas, jungle, bungalow, thug…

**4:16.0** — (savour it)
…and “*loot*”. [pause] They literally took the word for taking stuff.

**4:22.0**
And when languages collide, brand-new ones are born. In Papua New Guinea, English mixed with local tongues to create Tok Pisin…

**4:28.0** — (delighted)
…where a beard is “gras bilong fes” — grass of the face — and a hospital is a “haus sik”. [beat] Honestly? Better.

### 07 · LOST & FOUND
**4:35.5** — (softer, sincere)
But this isn’t only a story about winners. Hundreds of the Americas’ original languages are still alive — Quechua alone has around eight million speakers.

**4:43.0**
Guaraní is spoken by most of Paraguay, and Nahuatl — the language of the Aztecs — is still spoken in Mexico today.

**4:48.0** — (quiet, respectful)
Others weren’t so lucky. Sumerian, Etruscan, Gaulish — gone. And roughly every few weeks, another language loses its last speaker.

**4:55.0** — (lift, hopeful)
But languages can come back. Hebrew went from scripture to street talk. Welsh, Māori and Hawaiian are being taught to a whole new generation.

**5:03.0**
In the nineteen-eighties, fewer than fifty children spoke Hawaiian. [pause] Today there are *thousands*.

**5:09.0** — (playful again)
And Latin? Officially retired — but at the Vatican, the cash machines still ask you to insert your card… [beat] in Latin.

### 08 · THE SCOREBOARD
**5:18.5**
So where are we now? English leads in total speakers — about one and a half billion, most of whom learned it as a *second* language.

**5:26.0**
On native speakers, Mandarin wins — with Spanish in second place.

**5:31.0**
But the real language superpower is Papua New Guinea: about eight hundred and forty languages in one country — one for every twelve thousand people.

**5:38.0**
Indonesia, Nigeria and India each have hundreds more. Diversity didn’t disappear — [pause] it’s just quieter than the headlines.

### 09 · OUTRO
**5:46.5** — (warm, slow down)
So next time you order a coffee, eat a tomato or grab the shampoo, remember: [pause] every word is a souvenir from someone’s journey.

**5:53.5** — (smiling, one flavour per word)
Thank you. Gracias. Merci. Spasibo. Asante. Arigatō.
